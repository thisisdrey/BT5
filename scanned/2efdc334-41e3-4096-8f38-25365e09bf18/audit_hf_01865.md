# [M] M-10 rejectDepositor can be front-run

## Summary
Severity: Medium
Contest weight: 0.0277
Dataset id: 10395
Source: https://huggingface.co/datasets/Zaevlad/audit-findings-dataset
Type: audit-finding

## Details
The vulnerability is a front‑running race condition in the escrow contracts where the function that allows a buyer to reject a deposit (rejectDepositor) can be executed by the buyer after the seller has submitted a transaction to update the buyer’s status (updateBuyer) but before the seller’s transaction is mined. The root cause is that the contract does not enforce a proper ordering or atomicity between the seller’s acceptance of the buyer and the buyer’s ability to reject the deposit, allowing a malicious buyer to observe the pending seller transaction in the mempool and submit a rejectDepositor call that will be mined first. An attacker (typically the buyer) can monitor the network, detect the seller’s pending updateBuyer transaction, and then broadcast a rejectDepositor transaction with a higher gas price. Because Ethereum processes transactions in the order they are included in a block, the attacker’s transaction can be mined before the seller’s, causing the seller’s updateBuyer call to revert. When the seller’s transaction reverts, the seller loses the gas spent on the call and the escrow state does not progress, effectively resulting in a denial‑of‑service for the seller and potentially locking the escrowed funds until the issue is resolved. This situation occurs only when the seller attempts to finalize the buyer’s participation while the buyer remains able to invoke rejectDepositor, i.e., during the narrow window between the seller’s transaction being broadcast and its inclusion in a block. Both sellers and the overall protocol are affected because the escrow flow can be stalled, funds may remain locked, and users may experience unexpected transaction failures. The issue was discovered during a manual security audit that examined transaction ordering and state‑transition logic. It is hard to notice because the code appears to allow both parties to act independently, and the problem only manifests under specific timing conditions that are not evident from static analysis alone. To remediate the issue, the contract should enforce a mutual‑consent mechanism that prevents the buyer from calling rejectDepositor after the seller has signaled acceptance, for example by introducing a pause flag, a commit‑reveal scheme, or a mutex that locks the rejectDepositor function once updateBuyer has been called. From the user’s perspective, a seller may see their transaction revert with an “execution reverted” error and a loss of gas, while the buyer may see a deposit being rejected unexpectedly. The bug belongs to the class of front‑running or transaction‑ordering vulnerabilities that break business logic assumptions about the sequence of escrow operations, leading to funds appearing to disappear or escrow updates failing.

## Recommendation
We recommend allowing the seller to pause the updateBuyer function for such cases.
