## Details
The vulnerability resides in the resume routine of the pool contract, where an arithmetic miscalculation is performed when the pool is unpaused. The routine is expected to restore internal accounting variables—such as total deposited liquidity, accrued rewards, and user share ratios—to their correct values before normal operation resumes. Instead, the implementation uses an incorrect formula or fails to account for edge‑case values, causing the computed totals to diverge from the actual on‑chain balances. This discrepancy originates from a logical error in the arithmetic expression, for example an addition/subtraction order mistake, an omitted scaling factor, or a failure to handle integer rounding correctly. An attacker who can trigger the resume function (typically the pool owner or an authorized keeper) can exploit the bug by forcing the contract to accept a manipulated state where the recorded pool balance is lower than the real amount of tokens held. After the pool resumes, withdrawal calls are validated against the flawed internal total, allowing the attacker to withdraw more tokens than they are entitled to or to drain the pool while other users receive reduced or zero payouts. The impact is a loss of funds for all participants in the pool, incorrect reward distribution, and potential denial of service for honest users whose balances appear empty after the exploit. The condition for exploitation is that the pool must have been paused and then resumed; the bug manifests only during the state transition, making it invisible during normal operation and therefore hard to detect through routine functional testing. The issue was uncovered during a formal audit by Code4rena, where the auditors exercised the pause‑resume cycle and compared on‑chain token balances against the contract’s internal accounting, noticing a mismatch. Because the arithmetic error does not cause an immediate revert or event, it can remain hidden unless the pool’s accounting is scrutinized after each resume. To remediate the problem, the resume function should be rewritten to recompute all accounting variables using exact, overflow‑safe arithmetic, include proper scaling factors, and verify that the reconstructed state matches the actual token holdings before re‑enabling user interactions. The fix also benefits from adding assertions that compare the sum of individual user balances to the total pool balance after resumption, and from employing a well‑audited library for safe mathematical operations. In short, the bug belongs to the class of accounting‑logic errors caused by incorrect arithmetic during state transitions, leading to fund disappearance, incorrect refunds, and broken financial guarantees for pool participants.
