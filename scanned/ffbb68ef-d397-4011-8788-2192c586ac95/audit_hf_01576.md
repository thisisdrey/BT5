# [H] MJR-13 Calculation can be incorrect

## Details
The vulnerability is an accounting mis‑calculation in the pool’s liquidity logic where the variable that represents the expected liquidity of a pool is derived by adding the pool’s actual token balance to a “pseudo‑balance” that originates from borrowers’ accrued interest. This blending of real assets with interest that has not yet been realized causes the contract to over‑estimate the amount of funds that are actually available for liquidity providers (LPs) and other protocol operations. The root cause is the use of a single aggregate figure for expectedLiquidity that does not distinguish between on‑chain token holdings and off‑chain interest obligations, violating the fundamental accounting assumption that a pool’s reported liquidity must equal the sum of assets that can be immediately withdrawn. An attacker or a malicious borrower can trigger a situation where the protocol believes more liquidity exists than is truly present, allowing them to request withdrawals, swaps, or other actions that draw down the pool beyond its real balance. Because the excess amount is only a bookkeeping artifact, the protocol may later be unable to satisfy legitimate LP redemption requests, leading to missing or zero balances for users, unexpected shortfalls in refunds, or complete loss of funds for LPs. The issue manifests whenever borrowers accrue interest and the pool’s expectedLiquidity is consulted – for example during LP withdrawal, fee distribution, or forced closure of accounts. All participants who rely on the pool’s reported liquidity – LPs, borrowers, and the protocol itself – are affected. The flaw was discovered during a manual security audit that examined the PoolService contract and identified that the line computing expectedLiquidity mixes real balance with pseudo‑balance from borrowers’ interest. The problem is subtle because the numbers may appear reasonable on the surface; the over‑statement is hidden in the interest component, making it difficult to detect through simple balance checks. To remediate, the contract should separate the real token balance from any interest accruals, ensuring that expectedLiquidity reflects only assets that can be immediately transferred. Additionally, providing a mechanism to force‑close accounts and settle all outstanding balances for LPs would prevent the protocol from operating on inflated liquidity figures. In summary, the bug belongs to the class of “incorrect financial accounting” or “liquidity over‑estimation” vulnerabilities, where a contract’s internal accounting diverges from the actual on‑chain token holdings, leading to potential fund loss, failed refunds, and broken business logic.

## Recommendation
We recommend adding a function for forcing closing some accounts to pay all balance for LP.
