## Finding: Missing slippage protection (no minimum-received parameter) in `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`

### Title
Missing slippage/minimum-amount protection in `ExchangeInjectActuator` and `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` enables sandwich-attack fund loss for exchange creators - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
TRON's built-in bancor-style TRC10 `Exchange` supports three operations: create, inject (add liquidity), withdraw (remove liquidity), and transaction (swap). The swap operation (`ExchangeTransactionContract`) correctly includes an `expected` field that lets the caller specify a minimum-received amount, and `ExchangeTransactionActuator` enforces `anotherTokenQuant >= tokenExpected` before executing [1](#0-0) [2](#0-1) . In contrast, `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` have no equivalent minimum/expected field at all [3](#0-2) .

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` computes the counterpart token amount purely from the exchange's current pool balances at execution time, with no user-supplied bound: `anotherTokenQuant = floorDiv(multiplyExact(secondTokenBalance, tokenQuant), firstTokenBalance)` [4](#0-3) . The only sanity check in `doValidate()` is `anotherTokenQuant <= 0` [5](#0-4) , exactly analogous to the report's "amount received is not zero" bypassable check.

Similarly, `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()` computes `anotherTokenQuant` from the live pool ratio and only validates that it is `> 0` and within a rounding-precision tolerance (`"Not precise enough"`); it never lets the caller specify a minimum acceptable amount [6](#0-5) .

Because the pool ratio (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`) can be shifted by any other pending `ExchangeTransactionContract` (swap) transaction that gets ordered immediately before the inject/withdraw transaction in the same block, an attacker who observes a pending `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` in the mempool can:
1. Submit a swap that skews the pool ratio in their favor before the victim's inject/withdraw is applied.
2. Let the victim's inject/withdraw execute at the skewed ratio, causing the victim (only the exchange creator can call inject/withdraw, per the `creatorAddress` check [7](#0-6) ) to deposit/withdraw at an unfavorable rate.
3. Submit a reverse swap after, restoring the ratio and pocketing the difference — a classic sandwich attack, mirroring the reported Uniswap-V3 issue where `amount0Min`/`amount1Min` are zero.

### Impact Explanation
The exchange creator's TRX/TRC10 value is transferred to the attacker through the manipulated pool ratio during liquidity injection or withdrawal — this is a real, unauthorized loss of funds (economic theft) executable purely through ordinary signed transactions (`ExchangeTransactionContract` sandwiching `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract`), reachable by any account, without requiring privileged/malicious-SR behavior.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any account can create an `Exchange` and become its creator, and any other account can freely submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps against it. Front-running/sandwiching a visible pending transaction in the mempool via fee/ordering games is a standard, low-cost attack technique, making exploitation practical whenever an exchange creator injects or withdraws liquidity.

### Recommendation
Add a caller-specified minimum-received/maximum-cost field (analogous to `expected` in `ExchangeTransactionContract`) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()` before executing the balance changes.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes a pending `ExchangeWithdrawContract` from the exchange creator for exchange `E` (token pair `A/B`).
2. Attacker submits `ExchangeTransactionContract` swapping a large amount of `A` for `B` in `E`, shifting the pool ratio so `B` becomes relatively scarce/expensive.
3. The victim's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` executes against the skewed pool via `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()` [8](#0-7) , returning far less `B` than the ratio at broadcast time implied, since no minimum was specified.
4. Attacker submits a reverse swap restoring the ratio, capturing the value difference extracted from the victim. [9](#0-8) [10](#0-9)
