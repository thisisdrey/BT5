### Title
Missing Slippage Protection in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` Allows Front-Running of Bancor-style Pool Liquidity Withdrawals - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
`ExchangeWithdrawContract` lets an exchange pool creator withdraw `quant` of one token and receive a proportional amount of the paired token computed from the current pool balances at the moment the block containing the transaction is applied. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which carries an `expected` field enforcing a minimum-output check, `ExchangeWithdrawContract` has no such field, so the actuator always accepts whatever `anotherTokenQuant` the live pool ratio yields.

### Finding Description
In `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute`, the amount of the other token returned to the owner is computed purely from the current on-chain pool balances (`firstTokenBalance`, `secondTokenBalance`) and the requested `tokenQuant`, with no user-supplied lower bound: [2](#0-1) 

Compare this to `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, which validates that the computed `anotherTokenQuant` is not less than the caller-supplied `tokenExpected`: [3](#0-2) 

`ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate` performs range/precision checks (`exchange balance is not enough`, `Not precise enough`) but never bounds `anotherTokenQuant` against any value the withdrawer controls: [4](#0-3) 

Because the pool ratio (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`) can be shifted arbitrarily between the time the withdraw transaction is broadcast and the time the block including it is applied — via ordinary `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades or `ExchangeInjectContract` deposits from any other account in the same or an earlier block — an attacker who can order transactions relative to the victim's pending withdrawal (e.g., a block-producing witness, or simply a user submitting competing trades once the pending transaction is visible in the mempool) can manipulate the ratio to minimize the `anotherTokenQuant` the withdrawer receives, extracting the difference for themselves via a subsequent counter-trade. This mirrors the reported CurveSpell issue where `remove_liquidity` used `minOuts = 0`, exposing withdrawers to sandwich attacks with no way to bound their minimum acceptable output.

### Impact Explanation
An exchange-pool creator who withdraws liquidity can have their expected proceeds siphoned by a front-runner who trades against the pool immediately before the withdrawal is applied and reverses the trade afterward, extracting the spread. Because there is no `expected`/minimum-output parameter, the withdrawer has no on-chain mechanism to protect against this, resulting in a concrete theft of funds during withdrawal, analogous to the High severity finding in the external report.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any account can freely submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` transactions against the same exchange pool, and transaction ordering within a block is controlled by the block-producing witness (or can be influenced by anyone racing to get their trade included before/after the victim's withdrawal). Given the pool balances are public state, computing a profitable sandwich around a pending withdrawal is straightforward, making exploitation practical whenever a pool holds meaningful reserves.

### Recommendation
Add a minimum-output parameter (analogous to `expected` in `ExchangeTransactionContract`) to `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and enforce in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate`/`execute` that the computed `anotherTokenQuant` is not less than the caller-supplied minimum before mutating account and exchange state.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes a pending `ExchangeWithdrawContract` transaction from the pool creator withdrawing `tokenQuant` of `firstTokenID`.
2. Attacker submits an `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade that shifts `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` unfavorably for the withdrawer, ordered immediately before the withdrawal in the same block (or in a preceding block if they control block production or win the mempool race).
3. The victim's `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` computes `anotherTokenQuant` from the now-skewed ratio at lines 79-86 of `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`, yielding less than the victim would have received under the pre-attack ratio.
4. Attacker submits a second `ExchangeTransactionContract` reversing their trade, restoring the ratio and capturing the value taken from the victim's withdrawal.
