### Title
Meta-transaction relayer fund and gas siphoning via user-controlled `DelegateAction` receiver and deposit-refund routing - (File: runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs)

### Summary
NEAR's meta-transaction feature (NEP-366) has the same structural shape as the reported UNCX auto-collector issue: a semi-trusted third party (the relayer, analogous to `AUTO_COLLECT_ACCOUNT`) pays gas/token costs for an operation whose target contract, receiver, and actions are entirely chosen by an untrusted counterparty (the `DelegateAction` sender, analogous to the lock owner choosing tokens/position manager). Because the protocol routes balance refunds to the `sender_id` (the untrusted party) rather than the relayer who actually paid, and because account-initialization for the relayer's benefit is an off-chain trust step with no on-chain safeguard, a malicious sender can siphon or waste the relayer's attached balance and gas.

### Finding Description
A `DelegateAction` lets an account (`sender_id`) sign a set of `actions` and a `receiver_id`, both freely chosen by the sender, while a relayer wraps and submits it, paying all gas and any attached deposits [1](#0-0) . `apply_delegate_action` explicitly documents that "Relayer prepaid all fees and all things required by actions: attached deposits and attached gas. If something goes wrong, deposit is refunded to the predecessor, this is sender_id/Sender in DelegateAction. Gas is refunded to the signer, this is Relayer." [2](#0-1) 

This deposit-refund-to-predecessor behavior is a protocol-wide rule, not specific to delegate actions [3](#0-2) , but it becomes exploitable specifically in the meta-transaction context because the relayer, not the sender, is the one who actually funded the deposit. The architecture doc spells out the resulting attack directly: "The relayer could waste NEAR gas without compensation if Alice somehow reduces her $FT balance in just the right moment" [4](#0-3) , and more concretely: "there is a financial incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached but will fail on Bob's shard" so that the deposit refund routes back to her (the predecessor) instead of the relayer [5](#0-4) .

A second, related vector is documented for the account-bootstrapping trust step: a relayer must initialize an uninitialized account for the sender before that sender can construct a valid, nonce-checked `DelegateAction`. The docs state plainly that "if Alice wanted to abuse the relayer's helpful service... she does not sign a meta transaction, instead she deletes her account and cashes in the small token balance reserved for storage. If this attack is repeated, a significant amount of tokens could be stolen from the relayer." [6](#0-5) 

This is the same shape as the UNCX bug: a party subsidizes a costly on-chain interaction (`collect` / meta-transaction submission), the target/actions/receiver are user-controlled, and the payer has no on-chain protection against the counterparty steering value or gas expenditure away from them.

### Impact Explanation
A malicious `DelegateAction` sender can cause concrete, on-chain theft of relayer funds: (1) attached deposits on actions that are engineered to fail on the receiver's shard get refunded to the sender instead of the relayer who paid for them, and (2) storage-staking balances funded by a relayer for account bootstrapping can be withdrawn by the sender via `DeleteAccount` before ever executing a compensating meta-transaction. Both are direct loss-of-funds outcomes for the relayer, with no recourse once the receipts have settled.

### Likelihood Explanation
This requires only that some relayer accepts unauthenticated, arbitrary `SignedDelegateAction`s from third parties — the exact use case the feature is designed for. Nearcore's own documentation treats this as an accepted, currently-unmitigated trust assumption rather than a solved problem ("some trust is required" / "still needs to be some trust"), i.e., the protocol provides no on-chain guardrail and pushes the risk entirely to relayer operators, mirroring UNCX's acknowledged-but-unresolved posture.

### Recommendation
- Protocol-level: allow relayers to opt into routing deposit refunds for delegated actions to themselves (similar in spirit to the existing `promise_set_refund_to` mechanism [7](#0-6) ) instead of unconditionally refunding to `sender_id`.
- Consider removing/reducing the storage-staking cash-out incentive for accounts that were funded by a third party for meta-transaction bootstrapping, as the docs themselves suggest as a partial mitigation.
- Document/require relayer implementations to simulate delegate actions before submission and to rate-limit or bond first-time senders they bootstrap, since the protocol does not otherwise protect the payer.

### Proof of Concept
1. Relayer initializes account `alice` (funds an access key + minimal storage balance) so `alice` can construct a `DelegateAction` in the future, per the documented bootstrap workflow [8](#0-7) .
2. Instead of sending a meta-transaction, `alice` submits `DeleteAccount` directly, reclaiming the storage balance the relayer funded — no meta-transaction or relayer cooperation required after step 1.
3. Separately, `alice` signs a `DelegateAction` whose inner action attaches a large deposit to a call on `receiver_id` that `alice` knows will fail (e.g., a nonexistent method) [9](#0-8) . The relayer submits it and pays the deposit; because `apply_delegate_action` refunds the deposit to `sender_id` on failure [2](#0-1) , the funds return to `alice`, not the relayer, while the relayer has already paid out the deposit and gas up front.

```markdown
## Balance refunds in meta transactions

Unlike gas refunds, the protocol sends balance refunds to the predecessor
(a.k.a. sender) of the receipt. This makes sense, as we deposit the attached
balance to the receiver, who has to explicitly reattach a new balance to new
receipts they might spawn.

In the world of meta transactions, this assumption is also challenged. If an
inner action requires an attached balance (for example a transfer action) then
this balance is taken from the relayer.

The relayer can see what the cost will be before submitting the meta transaction
and agrees to pay for it, so nothing wrong so far. But what if the transaction
fails execution on Bob's shard? At this point, the predecessor is `Alice` and
therefore she receives the token balance refunded, not the relayer. This is
something relayer implementations must be aware of since there is a financial
incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached
but will fail on Bob's shard.
```
