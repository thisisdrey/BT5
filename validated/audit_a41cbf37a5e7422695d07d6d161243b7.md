### Title
Contract owner can front-run pending `TriggerSmartContract` calls by spiking `consume_user_resource_percent` via `UpdateSettingContract`, forcing victims to pay unexpected extra energy fees - ([File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/UpdateSettingContractActuator.java])

### Summary
`UpdateSettingContractActuator` lets a smart contract's origin/owner address change `consume_user_resource_percent` for their deployed contract at any time, with no timelock, cooldown, or restriction other than the value being in `[0, 100]`. This percent directly controls how much of the energy fee for a `TriggerSmartContract` call is charged to the calling user versus the contract owner. A malicious (or simply self-interested) contract owner can observe a victim's pending call transaction in the mempool and front-run it with an `UpdateSettingContract` transaction that raises the percent from a low/zero value up to 100, so the victim's already-broadcast transaction executes against the new setting and the victim is charged far more energy fee than they expected when they signed/broadcast the transaction — exactly the same "restricted-but-untrusted role changes a fee parameter to front-run a victim's pending transaction" bug class as the Sherlock TITLES royalty finding.

### Finding Description
`Edition.setRoyaltyTarget` in the referenced report is callable at any time by `EDITION_MANAGER_ROLE` and changes a fee-relevant parameter (royalty) that is read at the time a third party's transaction executes, with no commitment/delay, enabling front-running to harm a counterparty who already broadcast a transaction based on the old value.

The same shape exists in `UpdateSettingContractActuator`:
- `validate()` only checks that `owner_address` matches the deployed contract's `origin_address` and that the new percent is within `[0,100]`; there is no cooldown, no min/max delta, no time lock, and it can be executed unlimited times (`twiceUpdateSettingContract` test in `framework/src/test/java/org/tron/core/actuator/UpdateSettingContractActuatorTest.java:228-266` explicitly demonstrates the setting can be flipped back-to-back in immediate succession). [1](#0-0) 
- `execute()` immediately writes the new `consumeUserResourcePercent` into the `ContractCapsule` and evicts any cached repository entry, so the very next block/transaction that triggers the contract sees the new value. [2](#0-1) 
- The proto shows this field is a standalone, unrestricted, always-mutable setting on the deployed `SmartContract`: [3](#0-2) 
- `VMActuator` reads this percent when executing any `TriggerSmartContract` call to split the energy cost between the caller's own resources and the contract owner's `origin_energy_limit`/resources, i.e., it is consulted at the moment the victim's already-signed and broadcast transaction is applied, not at the moment the victim signed it. [4](#0-3) 

Because a Bob-equivalent victim signs and broadcasts a `TriggerSmartContract` transaction assuming the currently-observed `consume_user_resource_percent` (e.g. 0%, meaning the contract owner subsidizes energy), the contract owner can watch the mempool and front-run with `UpdateSettingContract` raising the percent to 100 before Bob's transaction is included, causing Bob's transaction to be charged the full energy cost from his own TRX/frozen-energy balance instead of the contract owner absorbing it. This mirrors the royalty case: an account holding a narrow, "restricted" administrative permission over a fee-affecting parameter can weaponize front-running against unsuspecting counterparties who already committed to a transaction under the old parameter value.

### Impact Explanation
A victim who broadcasts a `TriggerSmartContract` transaction expecting a specific energy-fee split can be forced, by the contract owner's front-run, to pay a much larger share (up to 100%) of the energy cost out of their own account, resulting in unexpected loss of TRX/energy resources for that account — a concrete unauthorized shift of costs onto an unprivileged transaction broadcaster, matching the "loss of assets for the victim" impact accepted as Medium in the source report. This is a systemic griefing/loss vector against every dApp caller, not merely an informational config change, because the parameter is read at execution time of transactions the victim did not control.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any deployed smart contract's owner already possesses the ability to call `UpdateSettingContract`; no elevated on-chain privilege beyond being the contract's `origin_address` is required, and the transaction has zero fee (`calcFee()` returns 0) and no rate limit, so a malicious contract owner can trivially monitor the mempool for calls to their own contract and repeatedly front-run any user transaction, exactly as described in the analog report.

### Recommendation
Introduce a delay/commit-reveal or cooldown window (e.g., only effective starting from the next maintenance cycle, similar to other dynamic parameter changes) before a `consume_user_resource_percent` (and `origin_energy_limit`) update takes effect, and/or cap how much a single update can move the percent, so pending transactions in the mempool cannot be front-run to retroactively change the fee split they will be charged.

### Proof of Concept
1. Contract owner deploys a contract `C` with `consume_user_resource_percent = 0` (owner pays all energy).
2. Bob observes this value, signs and broadcasts a `TriggerSmartContract` call to `C` expecting to pay ~0 energy fee himself.
3. The malicious owner sees Bob's pending transaction in the mempool and broadcasts `UpdateSettingContract{contract_address=C, consume_user_resource_percent=100}` with a higher fee/priority so it is included first (`UpdateSettingContractActuator.execute`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/UpdateSettingContractActuator.java:40-57`).
4. Once included, `ContractStore` reflects `consumeUserResourcePercent = 100` and the cache is invalidated.
5. Bob's transaction is then processed by `VMActuator`, which now reads the new 100% value and charges Bob's account the full energy cost instead of the expected 0%, causing an unanticipated loss of funds/resources for Bob — analogous to the royalty-front-run scenario in the source report.
