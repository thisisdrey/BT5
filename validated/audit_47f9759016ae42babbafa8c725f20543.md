### Title
Meta-transaction deposit refund routing lets an unprivileged signer grief a relayer's attached deposit - (File: runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs)

### Summary
In NEP-366 meta-transactions, the relayer (predecessor of the `Delegate`/`DelegateV2` action) fronts all gas and attached deposits for the inner actions signed by the sender. However, when the receiver-shard execution of those inner actions fails, the deposit refund is routed to the receipt's `predecessor_id`, which for the delegated receipt is the sender, not the relayer who actually paid. This lets an unprivileged sender construct a meta-transaction whose inner action(s) are engineered to fail on the receiver shard, so that the attached deposit — paid for by the relayer — is refunded back to the sender instead of the relayer, permanently transferring funds from the relayer to the sender.

### Finding Description
`apply_delegate_action` (`runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs:437-519`) builds a new `Receipt` on behalf of the `DelegateAction`'s inner actions with `predecessor_id: sender_id.clone()` [1](#0-0) , while the relayer (the original transaction's predecessor/signer for the outer `Delegate` action) is the one who actually pays for the attached deposit and gas of these inner actions, as stated explicitly in the code's own comment: "Relayer prepaid all fees and all things required by actions: attached deposits and attached gas. If something goes wrong, deposit is refunded to the predecessor, this is sender_id/Sender in DelegateAction." [2](#0-1) 

This is confirmed in the protocol's own documentation:
"Unlike gas refunds, the protocol sends balance refunds to the predecessor (a.k.a. sender) of the receipt... In the world of meta transactions... if an inner action requires an attached balance (for example a transfer action) then this balance is taken from the relayer. The relayer can see what the cost will be before submitting the meta transaction and agrees to pay for it, so nothing wrong so far. But what if the transaction fails execution on Bob's shard? At this point, the predecessor is `Alice` and therefore she receives the token balance refunded, not the relayer. This is something relayer implementations must be aware of since there is a financial incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached but will fail on Bob's shard." [3](#0-2) 

Mechanically, refunds are generated by `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` (`runtime/runtime/src/lib.rs:1166`) using `receipt.balance_refund_receiver()`, which resolves to the receipt's predecessor (the sender in the meta-tx case) rather than to whoever actually funded the deposit (the relayer) [4](#0-3) . The same predecessor-routing rule is used generally for deposit refunds across the runtime (see `docs/RuntimeSpec/Refunds.md:15-25`), but only in the meta-transaction flow is the entity who actually attaches/pays the deposit (the relayer) different from the receipt's `predecessor_id` (the sender) — this mismatch is the root cause of the griefing vector, mirroring the TraitForge pattern where a benefit/cost is tracked against one identity (`initialOwner`) while a different, unprivileged party (the current NFT holder) can trigger an action that permanently and unilaterally reduces value belonging to that first identity.

An unprivileged account (the meta-tx "sender"/Alice) can:
1. Sign a `DelegateAction` whose inner action(s) attach a deposit and target a receiver/method that Alice knows will fail (e.g., a nonexistent method, a contract that will panic given the actions' parameters, or a receiver account that doesn't exist).
2. Have this delegate action relayed on-chain by a trusting relayer who fronts the deposit believing it will either succeed (delivered to the intended receiver) or, if it fails, in the ordinary refund model be returned to whoever paid it.
3. When the inner action fails on the receiver shard, the deposit refund receipt is generated with `beneficiary` = sender (Alice), not the relayer, silently and permanently moving the relayer's funds to Alice.

### Impact Explanation
This is a concrete theft-of-funds primitive reachable purely by an unprivileged signer (the meta-transaction sender) crafting a transaction, with no special privileges needed beyond finding (or colluding with) a relayer willing to sponsor the meta-transaction. The relayer's attached deposit for the inner actions is provably and predictably redirected to the sender's account whenever the sender can force (or predict) failure of the inner action on the receiver shard, satisfying the "concrete theft ... of funds" bar. The nearcore documentation itself acknowledges the financial incentive this creates for a sender to intentionally submit meta-transactions with high attached balances that are engineered to fail.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is high for any general-purpose or semi-trusting relayer service: an attacker only needs their own account (no special stake/role) and to submit a `DelegateAction`/`DelegateV2` with an inner action attaching value to a receiver/method chosen to guarantee failure (e.g., calling a non-existent method or a method whose arguments deliberately violate a precondition). Because relayers must pre-approve/execute meta-transactions without necessarily being able to fully simulate all downstream failure conditions (cross-shard execution, contract-side reverts, storage-staking shortfalls on the receiver, etc.), this is realistically exploitable in production relayer deployments that sponsor deposits, not merely gas.

### Recommendation
Route deposit refunds for the inner-action receipt generated by `apply_delegate_action` to the relayer (the account that actually paid for gas/deposit — the outer transaction's signer/predecessor), not to `sender_id`, mirroring how gas refunds already correctly go to the relayer. Concretely, set the refund-target/`predecessor_id`-for-refund-purposes on the generated inner receipt (or add an explicit refund-beneficiary field, analogous to `promise_set_refund_to`) to the relayer identity captured from the original `Delegate` action's context, so that deposit refunds on execution failure return to whoever actually funded the deposit rather than to the meta-tx sender.

### Proof of Concept
Conceptual scenario (matches `test_meta_tx_execution` test harness pattern used in `integration-tests/src/tests/features/delegate_action.rs`):
1. Relayer funds and submits a `SignedTransaction` wrapping `Action::Delegate(SignedDelegateAction)` where `sender_id = Alice`, `receiver_id = Bob`.
2. Alice's `DelegateAction.actions` contains a `FunctionCall` to Bob with `deposit = 1000 NEAR` and `method_name = "non_existing_function"` (guaranteed to fail, similar to `test_refund_to` in `runtime/runtime/tests/test_async_calls.rs:1204-1297`, which already demonstrates the deposit-refund receipt being generated to a `predecessor_id`-derived beneficiary on failure).
3. On the receiver shard, the `FunctionCall` fails (method doesn't exist); `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` produces a `Receipt::new_balance_refund(receipt.balance_refund_receiver(), 1000 NEAR)` where the receipt's predecessor is Alice (per `apply_delegate_action`'s receipt construction at `runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs:484-497`).
4. Result: relayer's account balance decreases by 1000 NEAR (deposit) + gas, while Alice's balance increases by 1000 NEAR — net theft of the deposit from the relayer to Alice, even though the relayer never authorized sending funds to Alice.

Note: I was not able to directly execute or trace a live end-to-end integration test confirming the exact refund receiver field in the meta-tx failure path beyond the documented behavior and the `apply_delegate_action`/`refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` code paths cited above; this assessment relies on the explicit code comment and the project's own architecture documentation describing this exact scenario as an acknowledged risk.
