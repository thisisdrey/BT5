Confirmed analog found: `ExchangeInjectActuator` allows the exchange creator to inject liquidity at a proportional exchange rate computed live from `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`, with no user-supplied minimum-output ("expected") parameter to bound acceptable slippage — unlike `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, which does carry a `getExpected()` slippage guard checked at [1](#0-0) .

### Title
Missing slippage protection in ExchangeInjectActuator allows sandwich attacks against exchange creators' liquidity injections - (File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java)

### Summary
`ExchangeTransactionContract` includes a caller-specified `expected` field enforced in validation, effectively giving users slippage protection when swapping through a bancor-style `Exchange` pool [2](#0-1) . However `ExchangeInjectContract`, which lets the exchange creator inject a token amount and have a proportional `anotherTokenQuant` computed and debited from their own account based on the *current* pool ratio, has no analogous minimum/maximum bound field at all [3](#0-2) .

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate()` and `execute()` compute the counter-token amount required for injection strictly from the exchange's live `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` ratio at the moment the transaction executes: [4](#0-3) 

There is no user-supplied `expected`/limit parameter analogous to `ExchangeTransactionContract.getExpected()` to bound how much of the counter-token the creator is willing to deposit for a given amount of the first token. Because Tron transactions sit in the mempool/are ordered by block-producing witnesses before execution, an attacker (or the witness packing the block) can observe a pending `ExchangeInjectContract` transaction and execute `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps against the same `Exchange` pool immediately before it to shift `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`, then swap back immediately after. This changes the ratio used by `ExchangeInjectActuator` at execution time, forcing the injector to deposit a different (larger) amount of the counter-token than they intended for the same `tokenQuant`, while the attacker profits from the round-trip swap plus the ratio distortion captured during the injection. This mirrors the reported class of vulnerability: TopUpAction executed swaps with only a fixed default/maximum slippage bound and no user-specified limit, letting keepers/owners sandwich the swap; here `ExchangeInjectActuator` has *no* slippage bound whatsoever on the implied exchange rate used for the injected liquidity.

By contrast, `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` at least bounds the proportional withdrawal to a tight `0.0001` precision tolerance check [5](#0-4) , and `ExchangeTransactionActuator` has the explicit `expected` slippage guard, but `ExchangeInjectActuator` has neither.

### Impact Explanation
An exchange creator submitting `ExchangeInjectContract` can be sandwiched to receive an unfavorable exchange ratio, causing them to deposit more of the counter-token than intended for the injected amount, resulting in direct loss of funds to the attacker who captures the price movement via preceding/following `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps. Since balances moved are attacker/creator controlled TRC10 assets or TRX and the injector's account is debited according to the manipulated ratio, this is a concrete theft-of-funds vector reachable by any account holder that creates an exchange and any unprivileged account that can broadcast ordinary signed `ExchangeTransactionContract` transactions to manipulate the pool around the victim's transaction.

### Likelihood Explanation
Reaching this requires only standard signed transactions: an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap before and after a pending `ExchangeInjectContract`, both of which are ordinary externally-reachable actuators (`AbstractExchangeActuator` subclasses executed via normal transaction broadcast in `Manager`). No special privileges, witness/SR status, or contract deployment is needed—any account with tokens in the relevant `Exchange` pool can attempt it, and it is more attractive when the exchange creator injects large amounts relative to pool depth.

### Recommendation
Add a caller-specified bound (e.g., `expected`/`maxAnotherTokenQuant`) to `ExchangeInjectContract`, analogous to `ExchangeTransactionContract.getExpected()`, and enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()` so injections revert if the computed `anotherTokenQuant` exceeds the creator's specified tolerance for the current pool ratio.

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator broadcasts `ExchangeInjectContract` intending to inject `tokenQuant` of `firstToken`, expecting to pay approximately `anotherTokenQuant` of `secondToken` based on the current ratio.
2. Before this transaction is included, an attacker broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap that shifts `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` unfavorably for the injector (e.g., selling `secondToken` into the pool to raise its relative price).
3. The injector's `ExchangeInjectContract` executes against the manipulated ratio in `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` [6](#0-5) , causing the injector to deposit more `secondToken` than intended, with no `expected`-style check to reject the unfavorable computed quantity, since `doValidate()` never bounds `anotherTokenQuant` against a caller-supplied maximum [7](#0-6) .
4. Attacker broadcasts a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap immediately after, restoring the ratio and capturing the price difference as profit, extracted from the injector's excess deposit.
