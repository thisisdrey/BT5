### Title
Missing slippage/minimum-output protection in `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` enables sandwich price manipulation of the built-in Bancor exchange - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
The java-tron built-in Bancor-relay `Exchange` (TRC10 AMM) computes the ratio for `ExchangeInject` and `ExchangeWithdraw` purely from the pool's current `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` at execution time, with no minimum-expected-output parameter. `ExchangeTransactionContract` (a normal trade, callable by *any* unprivileged account) explicitly includes an `expected` field used to bound slippage [1](#0-0) , but `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` have no such field [2](#0-1) . This is the same root-cause class as the reported bug: a "spot pool balance at a single point in time" is used to compute the exchanged quantity, with no TWAP/oracle protection and no per-call slippage bound for the two operations that lack it.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeCapsule.transaction()` derives the exchanged amount solely from the pool's live `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` at the moment of execution [3](#0-2) . Anyone can shift this ratio arbitrarily (within the `balanceLimit` check) with an ordinary `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade, since that actuator is open to any account [4](#0-3) .

`ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()` compute `anotherTokenQuant` proportionally from the *current* pool ratio, with only a `"Not precise enough"` rounding check — there is no lower bound tied to what the withdrawer actually intended to receive [5](#0-4) . The same is true for `ExchangeInjectActuator`, which computes the proportional counterpart amount from the live ratio with no expected/minimum parameter at all [6](#0-5) .

Both `ExchangeInject` and `ExchangeWithdraw` are restricted to the exchange's creator address [7](#0-6) [8](#0-7) , but the pool ratio they read from is not creator-controlled — it is whatever balance the last `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade (submitted by any unprivileged account) left it in. An attacker can:
1. Front-run the creator's pending `Inject`/`Withdraw` transaction with a large `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade that skews the ratio to a value unfavorable to the creator's operation.
2. Let the creator's `Inject`/`Withdraw` execute at the skewed ratio (no minimum-out check exists to reject it).
3. Back-run with a reverse trade to restore the ratio and capture the value extracted from the creator's inject/withdraw, net of the small AMM fee.

This is the classic sandwich mechanic described in the report, adapted to java-tron: no flash loan is required because attacker capital just needs to move the pool within `getExchangeBalanceLimit()`, and the manipulation window is a single block (transaction ordering), fully reachable by an ordinary unprivileged transaction broadcaster.

### Impact Explanation
A successful sandwich forces the exchange creator's `Inject`/`Withdraw` to execute at a manipulated ratio, transferring value from the creator to the attacker — a concrete unauthorized transfer of TRX/TRC10 value between accounts via the exchange pool. Repeated sandwiching can drain meaningful value from exchange creators performing routine liquidity management, and because pool balances directly back other users' subsequent trades, it also distorts the price seen by other traders using `ExchangeTransactionContract` in between.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is moderate: the attack requires knowledge of a pending `Inject`/`Withdraw` transaction (observable in the mempool/pending pool) and capital sized relative to the pool (bounded by `getExchangeBalanceLimit()`), and it must be executed via transaction ordering within a block — feasible for any staked/funded account or SR-adjacent actor with ordering influence, and increasingly easy for low-liquidity exchanges where `balanceLimit` is easy to reach.

### Recommendation
Add an `expected`/minimum-output (and maximum-input) field to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, mirroring the protection already present in `ExchangeTransactionContract`, and enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` before mutating pool balances. Consider also enforcing a maximum single-block ratio deviation (analogous to `acceptableHighestPrice` in the referenced report) on `ExchangeTransactionActuator` itself, since it is the mechanism used to move the price that is later exploited against Inject/Withdraw callers.

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator broadcasts `ExchangeWithdrawContract` intending to withdraw `tokenQuant` of `firstTokenID` and receive proportional `secondTokenID` at the current 1:1-ish ratio.
2. Attacker observes this in the pending transaction pool and submits a large `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade of `secondTokenID` into the pool just before it, shifting `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` heavily (bounded only by `dynamicStore.getExchangeBalanceLimit()`) [4](#0-3) .
3. The creator's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` executes, computing `anotherTokenQuant` from the now-skewed ratio via `bigSecondTokenBalance.multiply(bigTokenQuant).divide(bigFirstTokenBalance)` [9](#0-8) , yielding far less `secondTokenID` than intended, with no on-chain check preventing it.
4. Attacker submits a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade to restore the ratio, capturing the value difference minus AMM fees.
