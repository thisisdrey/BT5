### Title
Unprotected liquidity injection into TRC10 Bancor-style exchange pool allows price-manipulation theft via front-running - (File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java)

### Summary
java-tron's built-in TRC10 exchange (`ExchangeCreateContract`/`ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeTransactionContract`) implements an on-chain bancor-formula liquidity pool. Adding liquidity to an existing pool via `ExchangeInjectActuator` computes the paired token amount from the pool's *current* live balances at execution time [3](#0-2) , with no caller-supplied minimum/maximum bound on the resulting `anotherTokenQuant`. This is structurally identical to the reported stNXM bug: adding liquidity without any slippage/price check lets an attacker manipulate the pool ratio right before the injection transaction lands, causing the injecting account to deposit into (or be forced to accept) a heavily skewed ratio.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` and `doValidate()` derive `anotherTokenQuant` purely from the pool's current `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` ratio and the caller's `tokenQuant`: [4](#0-3) 

Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract` (a trade), which carries an `expected` field enforced in validation (`anotherTokenQuant < tokenExpected` → reject) to provide slippage protection [5](#0-4) , `ExchangeInjectContract` only carries `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, and `quant` — there is no `expected`/minimum field [6](#0-5) .

Since any account can submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps against a public exchange pool (subject only to `getExchangeBalanceLimit()`), an attacker who observes a pending `ExchangeInjectContract` in the mempool can:
1. Front-run it with a large `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap that shifts the pool ratio.
2. Let the victim's inject transaction execute at the now-skewed ratio, causing the victim to inject a disproportionate amount of the paired token relative to what they intended (the pool computes `anotherTokenQuant` at execution time, not at signing time).
3. Back-run with a reverse swap to restore the ratio and extract the value effectively donated by the victim's mis-proportioned injection.

This mirrors exactly the reported root cause — "no slippage protection when minting [liquidity]" — applied here to java-tron's own AMM-like exchange rather than an external Uniswap pool.

### Impact Explanation
A liquidity provider (which could be an ordinary account, an exchange creator adding more depth, or even automated protocol logic built atop `ExchangeInjectContract`) can lose funds proportional to the degree of price manipulation achieved, with the attacker capturing the difference via the sandwiching trades. This is a concrete theft-of-funds vector reachable by any unprivileged account issuing standard, permission-less transactions (`ExchangeTransactionContract` and `ExchangeInjectContract`), qualifying as Medium/High severity depending on pool depth and available capital for the attacker's swap.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is meaningful but bounded: it requires (a) a pending `ExchangeInjectContract` visible in the mempool/pending pool, (b) sufficient attacker capital to swap enough volume to meaningfully move the ratio within the `getExchangeBalanceLimit()` cap, and (c) two of the attacker's own transactions ordered before/after the victim's tx in the same block or window — achievable by any block producer or MEV-capable participant, or simply by a fast bot when TRON's short block times allow same-block ordering influence.

### Recommendation
Add a minimum-output/slippage-bound field to `ExchangeInjectContract` (analogous to `expected` in `ExchangeTransactionContract`) and enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()`, rejecting the injection if the computed `anotherTokenQuant` falls outside the caller-specified acceptable range.

### Proof of Concept
Conceptual (adapted to `ExchangeInjectActuatorTest.java` harness):
1. Create/seed an exchange pool with `ExchangeCreateActuator` (balances `A0`, `B0`).
2. Victim constructs `ExchangeInjectContract` for token A with `quant = Q`, expecting `anotherTokenQuant ≈ (B0/A0)*Q` based on the pool state visible when they signed the tx, as demonstrated by the ratio computation in [7](#0-6) .
3. Attacker submits an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap that shifts the pool ratio before the victim's tx executes (both transactions land in the same or adjacent blocks, ordered by the attacker/block producer).
4. Victim's `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` now computes `anotherTokenQuant` from the manipulated ratio [8](#0-7) , forcing the victim to deposit far more of the paired token than fair value.
5. Attacker reverses the swap, recovering the manipulated ratio and pocketing the excess value the victim just injected.
