## Finding

### Title
Missing slippage protection in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` allows front-running of exchange liquidity operations - (File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java, actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java)

### Summary
The TRC10 bancor-style `Exchange` liquidity operations `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` compute the counterpart token amount (`anotherTokenQuant`) purely from the exchange pool's *current* balances at execution time, with no caller-specified minimum/maximum bound. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which includes an `expected` field used as a slippage check, the inject/withdraw contracts have no such protection, so a caller's transaction can be sandwiched by a preceding `ExchangeTransactionContract` (or another inject/withdraw) that shifts the pool ratio, causing the caller to receive far less (or give up far more) value than intended.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` only carry `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, and `quant` fields — no expected-amount/slippage field, as documented in the protocol spec: [1](#0-0) .

In `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()`, the amount of the paired token required from the owner (`anotherTokenQuant`) is derived from the exchange's live `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` at the moment of execution: [2](#0-1) . The same pattern exists in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()`, where the amount returned to the owner is computed from the pool ratio at execution time with no caller-provided bound: [3](#0-2) .

By contrast, `ExchangeTransactionActuator` explicitly validates a caller-supplied `tokenExpected` against the computed `anotherTokenQuant` and reverts if it is not met: [4](#0-3) . This shows the protocol designers were aware of the price-movement/front-running risk for trades but did not apply the same protection to inject/withdraw operations.

Any account can permissionlessly move the pool ratio by submitting an `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade against the same `exchange_id` right before a victim's `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` is packed into a block, or between the block containing the victim's broadcast and inclusion. Because the exchange creator (the only account allowed to inject/withdraw, per the "is not creator" check) has no way to bound their expected counterpart amount, the transaction executes at whatever distorted ratio exists when it runs — this is the direct protocol-native analog of the BribeCollector `claimFees()` front-running issue: a caller commits value expecting a market-implied return, but an attacker can shift the price/ratio in between commit and execution to extract value from (or force loss on) the caller.

### Impact Explanation
An attacker can sandwich an `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` transaction with `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades to manipulate the pool ratio, causing:
- On inject: the exchange creator deposits `tokenQuant` of one asset but is forced to also deposit an inflated `anotherTokenQuant` of the paired asset (computed at the manipulated ratio), losing value compared to the ratio they observed when signing.
- On withdraw: the exchange creator receives a deflated `anotherTokenQuant` for the `tokenQuant` withdrawn, losing expected value to the attacker who reverses their trade afterward and profits from both legs (classic sandwich/AMM front-run), extracting value at the victim's expense. This is a concrete theft-of-funds scenario reachable by any account submitting ordinary signed transactions.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is elevated relative to the BribeCollector report because TRON's transaction ordering within a block is controlled by the block-producing witness and pending transactions are visible in the mempool before confirmation, giving an attacker (or a colluding/observant party) the opportunity to insert `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades immediately before/after a known pending inject/withdraw transaction for the same `exchange_id`. No special privilege beyond being able to broadcast a signed transaction is required to execute the front-running leg.

### Recommendation
Add a caller-specified slippage bound (analogous to the `expected` field on `ExchangeTransactionContract`) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and validate/enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` before mutating balances, reverting the transaction if the computed `anotherTokenQuant` falls outside the caller's tolerance.

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator A holds an exchange with `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` at ratio R and broadcasts `ExchangeInjectContract` to inject `tokenQuant` of `firstToken`, expecting to also supply `anotherTokenQuant` of `secondToken` computed at ratio R (per client-side estimate), per `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` logic at [5](#0-4) .
2. Attacker B observes A's pending transaction and broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade against the same `exchange_id` that shifts the pool ratio, which the witness includes in an earlier position in the same or an earlier block.
3. A's `ExchangeInjectContract` then executes against the new, manipulated ratio, computing a different (larger) `anotherTokenQuant` than A expected, since there is no `expected`/slippage bound field to enforce A's assumption, unlike the check present in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` at [6](#0-5) .
4. B then reverses their trade, restoring the ratio and extracting the value difference from A's injected liquidity — the sandwich completes with A supplying more value than intended and B profiting.
