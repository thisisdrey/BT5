### Title
Missing slippage protection in `ExchangeInjectActuator` and `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` enables liquidity sandwich attacks - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
java-tron's Bancor-style TRC10 `Exchange` (AMM-like token-pair pool) exposes three broadcastable contract types: `ExchangeInjectContract` (add liquidity), `ExchangeWithdrawContract` (remove liquidity), and `ExchangeTransactionContract` (swap). Only the swap contract carries a caller-supplied `expected` minimum-output field that is validated in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` at [1](#0-0) . The inject and withdraw contracts have no equivalent slippage/expected parameter, as shown in the protobuf definitions [2](#0-1) , and both actuators compute the counter-token amount purely from the pool's current on-chain reserve ratio at execution time.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()`/`doValidate()` computes `anotherTokenQuant` as `secondTokenBalance * tokenQuant / firstTokenBalance` (or the inverse) using the exchange's live reserves, with no bound on how far that ratio may have moved since the transaction was signed: [3](#0-2) . `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` does the same for withdrawals, only adding a fixed-precision rounding check ("Not precise enough"), which is unrelated to price/ratio manipulation: [4](#0-3) . Both are reachable by any account address, subject only to being the exchange's `creatorAddress` [5](#0-4) , while the swap path (`ExchangeTransactionContract`) that would move the ratio is open to any unprivileged account with no such restriction [6](#0-5) . An attacker can therefore: (1) broadcast an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap that skews `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` immediately before the creator's pending inject/withdraw transaction lands in the same block, (2) let the creator's inject/withdraw execute at the skewed ratio and receive/contribute a mis-priced counter-token amount, then (3) broadcast a reverse swap to restore the ratio and pocket the difference — the classic liquidity-deposit/withdrawal sandwich described in the reference finding for Salty.IO's `formPOL`.

### Impact Explanation
A successful sandwich causes the exchange creator's inject to over-contribute one side of the pair (value transferred to the attacker) or causes a withdraw to receive a skewed, unfavorable split of the two TRC10 assets — a direct, unauthorized transfer of value from the liquidity provider to the attacker, extractable purely through public transaction broadcasting. This matches "theft of funds" impact criteria for an unprivileged actor exploiting normal API/transaction paths.

### Likelihood Explanation
The swap AMM math (`ExchangeProcessor.exchange`, using a Bancor relay-supply curve with exponents 0.0005/2000.0) does impose friction/cost on round-trip swaps [7](#0-6) , which somewhat limits (but, as the referenced Salty.IO case shows, does not eliminate) profitable sandwiches — profitability depends on pool depth, injected quantities, and relative curve slope. Exploitation requires only ordinary account transactions (a swap, then the victim's inject/withdraw, then a reverse swap) with no special privileges, and block producers or MEV-aware submitters can order transactions within a block to guarantee adjacency, making this practically reachable.

### Recommendation
Add an optional slippage-bound field (e.g., minimum/maximum `anotherTokenQuant`, or a maximum allowed reserve-ratio deviation) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and validate it in `ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate()`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()` analogous to the existing `tokenExpected` check in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` [1](#0-0) .

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes a pending `ExchangeInjectContract` (or `ExchangeWithdrawContract`) from the exchange creator address in the mempool, targeting exchange ID `E` with reserves `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`.
2. Attacker broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap on `E` to shift the ratio in their favor before the creator's transaction is packed into the block (or within the same block, ordered earlier).
3. The creator's `ExchangeInjectContract` executes via `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()`, computing `anotherTokenQuant` from the now-skewed reserves [8](#0-7) , causing the creator to deposit an unfavorable ratio of tokens.
4. Attacker broadcasts a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap to restore the ratio, extracting the value difference — no slippage check exists anywhere in the inject/withdraw path to block steps 2–4.
