The reported bug class—executing a trade based on stale AMM/pool ratios with no slippage bound, allowing intervening transactions to shift the ratio and cause the user to lose funds—maps cleanly onto java-tron's on-chain bancor-style Exchange contracts.

### Title
Front-runnable ratio change causes unbounded loss for `ExchangeInjectContract` / `ExchangeWithdrawContract` senders - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
`ExchangeInjectActuator` and `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` compute the counter-token amount (`anotherTokenQuant`) purely from the exchange pool's *current* on-chain balances at execution time, and neither exposes a minimum/maximum bound the caller can enforce. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which has an `expected` field checked in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` at [1](#0-0) , inject/withdraw offer no analogous protection.

### Finding Description
When a user (the exchange creator) submits `ExchangeInjectContract` or `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, they specify only `token_id` and `quant`; the "other side" amount is derived on-chain from the live pool ratio: [2](#0-1) 

Because `ExchangeTransactionContract` can be submitted by *any* account (no creator/ownership check is performed in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()`), an unprivileged transaction broadcaster can shift `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` of the pool between the time the victim signs/broadcasts their inject/withdraw transaction and the time it is actually included in a block, exactly mirroring the "state changes between submission and execution" root cause described in the external report. The victim's inject/withdraw transaction then executes against a completely different ratio than they intended, with no on-chain check that the computed `anotherTokenQuant` still matches their expectations: [3](#0-2) 

The only checks present are balance-limit and rounding-precision checks (`Not precise enough`), not price/ratio-bound checks: [4](#0-3) 

### Impact Explanation
- For `ExchangeInjectContract`: the creator can be forced to deposit a much larger amount of the counter-asset than intended (or receive a worse ratio), directly losing funds to whoever manipulated the ratio beforehand.
- For `ExchangeWithdrawContract`: the creator can be forced to withdraw far less of the counter-asset than the ratio they observed when signing, permanently losing the difference to the pool (and, by extension, to whoever trades against the pool afterward).

This is a direct funds-loss vector reachable purely through normal, signed transactions (`ExchangeTransactionContract`, `ExchangeInjectContract`, `ExchangeWithdrawContract`)—no special privilege is required to trigger the front-run, only to be the exchange creator to suffer the specific inject/withdraw loss.

### Likelihood Explanation
Medium: it requires an attacker to observe a pending inject/withdraw transaction (e.g., in the mempool/broadcast layer) and race an `ExchangeTransactionContract` ahead of it, or simply requires normal trading activity/network delay to coincide with the creator's inject/withdraw call. No special permissions are needed to submit the front-running `ExchangeTransactionContract`.

### Recommendation
Add an `expected`-style bound to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` (e.g., `expected_another_amount` with min/max semantics depending on direction), and validate it in `ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate()` / `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()` the same way `tokenExpected` is validated in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` at [1](#0-0) .

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes creator `C` broadcasting `ExchangeWithdrawContract` for `exchangeId=X`, `tokenId=firstTokenId`, `quant=Q`.
2. Attacker immediately broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` on the same exchange with a large `quant` of `secondTokenId`, which is unprivileged and processed by `ExchangeTransactionActuator.execute()` (no creator check), shifting `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` heavily.
3. If the attacker's transaction is included first, `C`'s withdraw is then processed by `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()` against the new, skewed ratio, computing a much smaller `anotherTokenQuant` than `C` expected when they signed the transaction, per [5](#0-4) .
4. `C` permanently loses the difference between the expected and actual counter-token amount, with no on-chain mechanism to reject the unfavorable execution.
