## Title
Missing slippage protection in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` exposes exchange liquidity providers to sandwich attacks - ([File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java])

### Summary
`ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` — the java-tron equivalents of "minting/burning" liquidity in the built-in Bancor-style TRX exchange — carry no minimum/maximum-counterpart-amount parameter. In contrast, `ExchangeTransactionContract` (the trade/swap operation) explicitly carries an `expected` field that is validated against the computed output before execution. Because inject/withdraw recompute the counterpart amount purely from the pool's live reserves at execution time with no bound accepted by the caller, an unprivileged trader can sandwich a pending inject/withdraw transaction with ordinary `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades to force the liquidity operation to execute at a manipulated price, exactly the "unlimited slippage on mint/burn" bug class described in the source report.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeTransactionActuator` protects traders from slippage: the contract includes an `expected` field [1](#0-0) , which is checked in `doValidate()` — `anotherTokenQuant < tokenExpected` throws `"token required must greater than expected"` [2](#0-1) .

`ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, however, only carry `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, `quant` — no analogous minimum/maximum field [3](#0-2) .

`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` computes the counterpart amount to be paid in directly from the pool's current reserves at the moment of execution, with no bound supplied by the caller: [4](#0-3) 
The validation step performs the identical unbounded computation and only checks `anotherTokenQuant <= 0`, never comparing it to any caller-supplied minimum/maximum: [5](#0-4) 

`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` has the same structure — the amount of the counterpart token the creator receives is computed live from reserves with no floor the caller can enforce: [6](#0-5)  and its `doValidate()` only checks internal consistency ("exchange balance is not enough", "withdraw another token quant must greater than zero", "Not precise enough"), never a minimum acceptable output chosen by the withdrawer: [7](#0-6) .

Because `ExchangeTransactionContract` is a plain, permissionless, broadcastable contract that any account can submit to trade against any exchange pool (it only requires balance and validates against `firstTokenID/secondTokenID`) [8](#0-7) , an attacker can:
1. Submit a large trade to shift `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` right before the victim's pending `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` transaction lands (in the same block, since transactions execute in the order they are packed, and an attacker with sufficient bandwidth/energy or a block-producing witness can order transactions favorably).
2. The victim's inject/withdraw executes against the manipulated ratio, causing them to deposit far more of one asset than expected relative to the other, or to receive far less of the counterpart token when withdrawing.
3. Attacker reverses the trade afterward, extracting the value the victim overpaid/undereceived, walking away with the difference — a mechanically identical attack to the "unlimited slippage" bug described in the L2ECOBridge report, just applied to java-tron's on-chain Bancor exchange instead of an L2 bridge mint/burn.

### Impact Explanation
This directly causes loss of funds for the exchange creator/liquidity-provider account performing an inject or withdraw operation: they can be forced into unfavorable liquidity actions with no way to bound their exposure, since neither contract exposes a slippage/limit parameter and neither actuator enforces one. This is a concrete unauthorized-value-extraction vector against a specific account's assets during a normal, permissionless workflow (exchange creation + injection/withdrawal alongside third-party trades), matching the "High" severity fund-loss criteria.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is high wherever exchanges have moderate liquidity and predictable owner behavior (e.g., automated LP rebalancing bots that periodically inject/withdraw): any unprivileged account can submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades to manipulate `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` immediately around a known pending inject/withdraw transaction. No special privileges, precompiles, or contract deployment are required — only ordinary broadcast transactions against the public exchange actuators.

### Recommendation
Add an `expected`/limit field (minimum counterpart amount received for withdraw, maximum counterpart amount paid for inject) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, mirroring the existing `expected` field and check already implemented in `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, and enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator`'s `doValidate()`/`execute()` before mutating balances.

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator submits `ExchangeInjectContract` (or `ExchangeWithdrawContract`) for exchange `X`, expecting the counterpart amount implied by the pool's current ratio.
2. Before this transaction is applied, an attacker (any account) submits an `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade against exchange `X` that shifts `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` significantly.
3. The creator's inject/withdraw actuator (`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute()` / `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()`) recomputes `anotherTokenQuant` from the now-manipulated reserves [4](#0-3) , executing at a far worse ratio than the creator anticipated, with no validation to reject it since no minimum/maximum field exists on the contract or is checked in `doValidate()`.
4. Attacker submits a reverse trade to restore the pool ratio, capturing the value difference extracted from the creator's inject/withdraw.
