## Title
`ExchangeInjectActuator` computes counterpart-token amount from spot pool ratio with no caller-supplied minimum/maximum bound, enabling sandwich extraction of victim funds - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`)

### Summary
`ExchangeInjectActuator` lets the creator of a TRC10 `Exchange` (java-tron's on-chain AMM/bancor-style pair) add liquidity by specifying only the quantity of one token; the amount of the *other* token that is automatically debited from the caller's account is derived from the pool's instantaneous balance ratio at execution time, with no minimum/maximum bound supplied in the transaction. This mirrors the reported `TrufPartner.pay` bug class: an add-liquidity operation whose required counterpart amount is computed from a manipulable, on-chain, at-execution-time ratio, without a slippage-protection parameter, so it can be sandwiched.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectContract` only carries `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, and `quant` — there is no `expected`/min or max bound field, unlike its sibling `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which does carry an `expected` field used to protect trades (`ExchangeTransactionContract` proto and its check at [1](#0-0) ).

In `ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()`, the amount of the paired token (`anotherTokenQuant`) that will be pulled from the caller's balance is computed purely from the current pool balances at the moment the transaction executes: [2](#0-1) 

The same unconditional proportional-ratio computation, again derived only from balances at execution time with no caller-supplied bound, occurs in `execute()`: [3](#0-2) 

Because any unprivileged account can submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades against the same `exchange_id` (the TRC10 exchange is a public AMM pair, not access-restricted like `TrufPartner`), an attacker who observes a pending `ExchangeInjectContract` in the mempool can:
1. Front-run it with a large `ExchangeTransaction` swap that skews the pool's first/second token balance ratio so that `anotherTokenQuant` computed for the victim's injection becomes maximally unfavorable to the victim (i.e., the victim is forced to contribute far more of the paired token than they intended, relative to the token amount they explicitly specified).
2. Let the victim's `ExchangeInject` execute at that skewed ratio, deducting the inflated `anotherTokenQuant` from the victim's account balance/asset holdings via `reduceAssetAmountV2`/`setBalance` (execute() lines above).
3. Back-run by reversing the initial swap, restoring the pool ratio and recovering the swapped token, net of trading fees — capturing the excess value that was pulled from the victim as profit.

This is the direct analog of the reported issue: `TrufPartner.pay` adds Uniswap liquidity with `amountBMin = 0`, so the amount of `pairToken` actually pulled from the subscription owner is dictated by whatever ratio exists at execution time, letting a sandwich attacker manipulate that ratio for profit. `ExchangeInjectActuator` has the identical structural flaw — a liquidity-provision amount derived from a spot ratio with zero minimum/maximum protection — except the "add liquidity" call here is a first-class java-tron transaction type reachable by any signed transaction against a public, permissionless AMM pair.

### Impact Explanation
An attacker can extract value directly from any account that submits an `ExchangeInjectContract` against a TRC10 `Exchange` pair, by sandwiching it with `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades. The victim's account is debited (via `reduceAssetAmountV2` for TRC10 assets or `setBalance` for TRX) an amount of the counterpart token that is inflated relative to what they intended to contribute, and this value is transferred to the attacker net of AMM trading fees. This is a concrete unauthorized transfer/theft of funds from any unprivileged user who calls `ExchangeInject`, reachable purely by broadcasting ordinary signed transactions (`ExchangeTransactionContract` and `ExchangeInjectContract`) — no special privilege is required by attacker or victim.

### Likelihood Explanation
`Exchange`-type TRC10 pairs and the `ExchangeInject`/`ExchangeTransaction` contract types are part of the standard, long-supported java-tron transaction set, fully reachable from any client via the normal broadcast path. Any account can create/observe pending transactions referencing a given `exchange_id` and race them with `ExchangeTransactionContract` swaps in the same or adjacent blocks, since the actuator provides no protection against ratio manipulation between transaction submission and inclusion.

### Recommendation
Add a caller-specified bound (e.g., `expected_another_token_quant_max` or similar) to `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract`, mirroring the `expected` field already present in `ExchangeTransactionContract`, and have `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` validate the computed `anotherTokenQuant` against that bound before executing, reverting if the ratio has moved beyond the caller's tolerance.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes a pending `ExchangeInjectContract` from victim `V` for `exchange_id=E`, injecting `tokenQuant` of `firstTokenID`.
2. Attacker submits an `ExchangeTransactionContract` on `E` swapping a large amount of `secondTokenID` for `firstTokenID`, driving `firstTokenBalance` up and `secondTokenBalance` down (see ratio math at `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeTransactionActuator.java` lines 149-238 for the analogous public trade path).
3. Victim's `ExchangeInject` executes; `anotherTokenQuant = secondTokenBalance * tokenQuant / firstTokenBalance` is now computed against the skewed, low `secondTokenBalance`/high `firstTokenBalance` ratio, but because `secondTokenBalance` is now artificially low, per the code at [4](#0-3)  the victim is charged a distorted `anotherTokenQuant` relative to fair-market value, deducted from V's account.
4. Attacker submits the reverse `ExchangeTransaction` swap on `E`, restoring the ratio and recovering `firstTokenID`, netting a profit from the ratio distortion minus trading fees, at V's expense.
