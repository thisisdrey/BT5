### Title
Missing Minimum-Output Slippage Protection in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` - (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
The `ExchangeWithdrawContract` used to remove liquidity from a TRON Bancor-style exchange pair has no field allowing the withdrawer to specify a minimum acceptable amount of the "other" token to receive. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which carries an `expected` field enforced during validation, `ExchangeWithdrawContract` computes and pays out `anotherTokenQuant` unconditionally, with no caller-supplied floor.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeWithdrawContract` is defined with only `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, and `quant` — no minimum/expected parameter: [1](#0-0) 

In `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute`, the counter-token amount `anotherTokenQuant` is derived purely from the current on-chain pool ratio (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`) at execution time, and is paid to the withdrawer without any check against a caller-specified minimum: [2](#0-1) 

`doValidate()` only checks that quantities are positive and that the exchange balance is sufficient and "precise enough" — it never compares the computed `anotherTokenQuant` to any value the caller controls: [3](#0-2) 

By contrast, the sibling actuator `ExchangeTransactionActuator` (a trade rather than a withdrawal) explicitly requires a caller-supplied `expected` floor and reverts with `"token required must greater than expected"` if the trade would yield less: [4](#0-3) 

This asymmetry is the exact analog of the reported bug class: `BalancerAuraDestinationVault._burnUnderlyer` hardcodes `minAmounts` to `0` during liquidity removal while other paths in the same protocol support slippage bounds. Here, `ExchangeWithdrawContract` has no minimum-amount field at all for its liquidity-removal operation, even though the same actuator family (`ExchangeTransactionContract`) demonstrates that the protocol design intends slippage floors to be enforceable.

### Impact Explanation
Because the pool ratio used to compute `anotherTokenQuant` is read at execution time (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` at block-processing time, not submission time), the amount the exchange creator receives is not deterministic when they sign and broadcast the `ExchangeWithdrawContract` transaction. Between the moment a withdrawal transaction is broadcast and the moment it is packed into a block, other participants can broadcast `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeTransactionContract` transactions that shift the pool ratio (front-running/sandwiching), causing the withdrawer to receive a smaller-than-expected amount of the second token with no on-chain mechanism to reject the unfavorable execution. This is a concrete, unauthorized value-extraction vector against the exchange creator's own withdrawn liquidity — funds loss due to unbounded slippage.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any account that is the creator of an exchange pair can call `ExchangeWithdrawContract` at any time via a normal signed transaction; this requires no special privilege beyond having created the exchange (an ordinary, unprivileged, permissionless action available to any account, analogous to an "order placer"). An adversary observing the mempool/broadcast transaction can trivially inject a manipulating trade in the same block-inclusion window, since `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeTransactionContract` are also unprivileged and cheap to submit.

### Recommendation
Add a minimum-expected-amount field to `ExchangeWithdrawContract` (analogous to `expected` in `ExchangeTransactionContract`), and enforce it in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()` by rejecting the withdrawal if the computed `anotherTokenQuant` (or the primary `tokenQuant` payout, if partial withdrawal ratios can vary) falls below the caller-supplied floor.

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator `A` owns exchange pair `(X, Y)` with balances `X=100000000`, `Y=200000000`.
2. `A` broadcasts `ExchangeWithdrawContract{exchange_id, token_id=X, quant=1000000}`, expecting `anotherTokenQuant ≈ 2000000` based on the ratio at submission time.
3. Before `A`'s transaction is included, attacker `B` broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` that sells a large amount of `Y` into the pool, shifting the ratio unfavorably for `Y` withdrawals.
4. When `A`'s withdrawal is finally processed by `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` ( [5](#0-4) ), `anotherTokenQuant` is recomputed against the new, manipulated ratio and paid out — significantly less `Y` than `A` expected, with no validation step to reject the unfavorable outcome, since no minimum field exists on the contract to check against.
