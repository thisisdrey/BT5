### Title
Exchange Inject/Withdraw Have No Slippage Protection, Enabling Front-Run Manipulation of the LP Ratio - ([File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java], [File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java])

### Summary
`ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` — the TRC10 bancor-style AMM liquidity operations — only let the caller specify the quantity of one token (`quant`). The proportional amount of the paired token (`anotherTokenQuant`) is derived at execution time from whatever the live pool ratio happens to be, with no caller-supplied minimum/maximum bound. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which carries an `expected` field used as slippage protection [1](#0-0) , neither `ExchangeInjectActuator` nor `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` exposes any such bound.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute` computes `anotherTokenQuant` purely from the exchange's current `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` ratio at the moment the transaction is processed, and then debits that computed amount from the caller's account: [2](#0-1) . The validate step recomputes the same ratio-derived amount and only checks balance sufficiency, never a caller-supplied expectation [3](#0-2) .

Similarly, `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` computes `anotherTokenQuant` from the live pool ratio and credits that amount back to the creator: [4](#0-3) . Its validate method only performs a rounding "precise enough" sanity check against its own recomputation of the ratio — it does not compare against any value the caller committed to when signing the transaction [5](#0-4) .

Because the pool ratio can be shifted arbitrarily by anyone submitting an `ExchangeTransactionContract` (open to any account with sufficient balance, see `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate`) [6](#0-5) , an unprivileged attacker who observes a pending `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` in the mempool can front-run it with a trade that skews `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`, then back-run it to restore the ratio (a classic sandwich). This is exactly the bug class described in the external report: push/pull-style vault operations that compute a paired amount from live state without an `options`/minimum-amount parameter for slippage protection.

### Impact Explanation
The victim is the exchange's creator (the only account authorized to call Inject/Withdraw, per the creator check in both actuators [7](#0-6) ). By sandwiching the creator's `ExchangeInjectContract`, an attacker can force the creator to pay far more of the paired token than the ratio at submission time implied. By sandwiching an `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, an attacker can force the creator to receive far less of the paired token than expected. Either way this is a concrete transfer of value from the victim to the attacker (theft of funds via price manipulation), satisfying the "unauthorized ... theft of funds" impact bar.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any account can submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` transactions with no special permission, and TRON's transaction ordering within a block (fee-less, first-come priority among broadcast transactions) makes sandwiching a pending Inject/Withdraw transaction from the mempool straightforward for a motivated actor once they see it broadcast. The precondition is simply that an exchange creator submits an Inject/Withdraw transaction, which is routine exchange-management activity.

### Recommendation
Add a caller-supplied bound (e.g., `expected`/min-`anotherTokenQuant` or min/max acceptable ratio) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and enforce it in `ExchangeInjectActuator.doValidate`/`execute` and `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate`/`execute` analogous to the `tokenExpected` check already present in `ExchangeTransactionActuator` [1](#0-0) .

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator broadcasts `ExchangeWithdrawContract` for `exchangeId=X`, `tokenId=A`, `quant=Q`, expecting to receive `anotherTokenQuant` computed from the current pool ratio `secondTokenBalance/firstTokenBalance`.
2. Attacker observes this pending transaction and front-runs it with an `ExchangeTransactionContract` that sells a large amount of token A into the pool, sharply reducing `secondTokenBalance` relative to `firstTokenBalance` (per `ExchangeCapsule.transaction`).
3. The victim's withdraw executes against the now-skewed ratio (`ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute`, lines 77-89), returning a much smaller `anotherTokenQuant` than the creator anticipated.
4. Attacker back-runs with an opposite `ExchangeTransactionContract` to restore the ratio and lock in the difference, having extracted value from the creator's withdrawal with no slippage check to prevent it.
