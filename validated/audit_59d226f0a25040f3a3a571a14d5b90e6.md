Confirmed: `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` proto definitions have no `expected`/minimum field, unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract` which explicitly has `expected` for slippage protection. [1](#0-0) 

### Title
Sandwichable Bancor-exchange liquidity withdraw/inject with no slippage protection leads to loss of funds - (File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java, actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java)

### Summary
`ExchangeWithdrawContract` and `ExchangeInjectContract`, the liquidity-withdraw and liquidity-inject operations of java-tron's built-in Bancor-style TRX/TRC10 exchange, expose no user-controllable minimum-output ("slippage") parameter, unlike the trading operation `ExchangeTransactionContract` which explicitly carries an `expected` field. This mirrors the Superposition H-03 finding: a withdrawal (or deposit) path that computes a pro-rata output purely from the live pool ratio, with no way for the caller to bound the acceptable output, is inherently sandwichable.

### Finding Description
`ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` computes the amount of the paired token (`anotherTokenQuant`) to return to the caller strictly from the current on-chain pool ratio (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`) at execution time: [2](#0-1) 

Its validator only checks that the derived amount is non-negative, within bounds, and "precise enough" relative to a hardened rounding tolerance — it never compares against any value chosen by the transaction submitter: [3](#0-2) 

The `ExchangeWithdrawContract` protobuf message itself has no minimum-output field at all — only `owner_address`, `exchange_id`, `token_id`, and `quant`: [4](#0-3) 

The same structural gap exists for `ExchangeInjectActuator`, which computes the paired-token amount the depositor must pay in purely from the live ratio with no ceiling the caller can enforce: [5](#0-4) 

By contrast, `ExchangeTransactionActuator` (a trade, not a withdraw/deposit) does carry and enforce an `expected` minimum-output field: [6](#0-5) [7](#0-6) 

This asymmetry means the exchange's trading path is slippage-protected while its liquidity-management paths are not. Any account can become the "creator" of an exchange (and therefore the only account authorized to withdraw from it) simply by broadcasting `ExchangeCreateContract`, so the victim of a sandwich on `ExchangeWithdrawContract` is an ordinary, unprivileged network participant, not a privileged validator role: [8](#0-7) 

An attacker observing a pending withdraw (or inject) transaction can broadcast an `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade immediately before it to skew `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` in their favor, let the victim's withdraw/inject execute at the manipulated ratio, then broadcast a reverse trade afterward to restore the ratio and capture the difference — a classic sandwich attack, with no on-chain mechanism available to the victim to prevent it.

### Impact Explanation
A successful sandwich causes the withdrawing/injecting account to receive materially less (or pay materially more) of the paired asset than the pool ratio implied when they signed and submitted their transaction, resulting in a direct, permanent, and irreversible loss of TRX/TRC10 asset value to the attacker. This satisfies the "theft of funds" impact class.

### Likelihood Explanation
Exploitation only requires an unprivileged account to (a) create or control an exchange, or observe someone else's pending withdraw/inject, and (b) submit ordinary, permissionless `ExchangeTransactionContract` trades before and after the target transaction — no special privileges, node access, or protocol bugs beyond the missing slippage check are needed. The larger the exchange's liquidity imbalance an attacker can create relative to pool depth, the larger the extractable profit, making this practically exploitable against any exchange with non-trivial liquidity and infrequent trading (making manipulation cheap).

### Recommendation
Add an explicit minimum/maximum bound field to `ExchangeWithdrawContract` and `ExchangeInjectContract` (analogous to the `expected` field on `ExchangeTransactionContract`), and enforce it in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator`/`ExchangeInjectActuator` validation, rejecting the transaction if the computed `anotherTokenQuant` falls outside the caller-specified bound.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker monitors the mempool/pending transactions and observes a pending `ExchangeWithdrawContract` from victim V, withdrawing `tokenQuant` of `firstTokenID` from exchange `E`, expecting `anotherTokenQuant` of `secondTokenID` based on the current ratio.
2. Attacker submits `ExchangeTransactionContract` swapping a large amount of `secondTokenID` into `E` for `firstTokenID`, shifting the ratio so that `secondTokenBalance` is depleted relative to `firstTokenBalance`.
3. V's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` executes at the now-skewed ratio in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` (lines 74-89), returning far less `secondTokenID` than V expected, since the code has no way to reject execution based on a minimum output.
4. Attacker submits a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade, restoring the ratio and extracting the value difference as profit, while V's transaction succeeded with `code.SUCESS` but at an unfavorable rate they had no way to guard against.
