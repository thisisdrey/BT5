## Finding

### Title
On-chain Exchange spot price used as unprotected oracle enables single-transaction AMM price manipulation and fund extraction - (File: `chainbase/src/main/java/org/tron/core/capsule/ExchangeCapsule.java`)

### Summary
TRON's built-in TRC10 `Exchange` (Bancor-relay AMM) derives its trade price purely from the two on-chain reserve balances stored in the `Exchange` capsule, recalculated instantaneously on every call with no time-weighting, no minimum-liquidity floor, and no protocol-enforced maximum price-impact. Any unprivileged account can call `ExchangeTransactionActuator` to execute an arbitrarily large trade against a chosen `Exchange`, skewing the reserve ratio (the pool's "price") within a single transaction, and reverse it shortly after — the same bug class ("price oracle manipulation via low-liquidity/manipulable spot price") that caused the Rodeo Finance loss.

### Finding Description
The exchange rate is computed on the fly from `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` stored in `ExchangeCapsule`, via `ExchangeCapsule.transaction()` which calls either `ExchangeProcessor.exchange()` or `SafeExchangeProcessor.exchange()`: [1](#0-0) 

The relay/Bancor curve itself only depends on the current reserve balances passed in for that single call — there is no accumulator, no TWAP, no external reference price: [2](#0-1) 

Any account can invoke a trade via `ExchangeTransactionActuator.execute`, which reads the current pool balances and immediately settles at the resulting curve price, then persists the new (attacker-skewed) balances back to the store: [3](#0-2) 

The only slippage guard is `tokenExpected`, a minimum-receive value chosen by the trader themselves, checked in `doValidate()`; there is no protocol-side maximum-price-impact or per-block trade-size cap: [4](#0-3) 

By contrast, `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` are restricted to the pool creator and preserve the existing ratio (proportional liquidity add/remove), so they cannot be used to move price: [5](#0-4) 

This means the *only* mechanism that moves the pool's spot price is `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, which is open to any signer. An attacker can:
1. Submit a large `ExchangeTransactionContract` that sells a large `tokenQuant` of one side, driving the reserve ratio far from the fair/external market price within a single transaction.
2. Have any dependent logic or counter-party (another trader who queries `getMarketPriceByPair`/trades against the same pool in an adjacent transaction) execute against the now-skewed instantaneous price.
3. Submit a second `ExchangeTransactionContract` reversing the initial trade, extracting the value taken from the counter-party/LP at the expense of the pool's actual token reserves (which back other participants' claims), then withdraw the resulting excess balance.

This is architecturally identical to the Rodeo Finance root cause: a price feed that is nothing more than a spot ratio inside a shallow, permissionlessly tradable pool, with no manipulation resistance (TWAP, liquidity depth checks, or circuit breakers), reachable by any unprivileged transaction sender.

### Impact Explanation
Exploiting this allows an attacker to extract real token/TRX value backed inside a specific `Exchange` pool from other legitimate participants (the pool creator's injected liquidity and/or any trader who transacts against the pool while the price is skewed), i.e., concrete theft of funds. Because `Exchange` balances are on-chain TRC10/TRX reserves actually owned collectively by whoever put liquidity/trades into that pool, extracting more value than fair-market rate constitutes unauthorized transfer of value away from other pool participants.

### Likelihood Explanation
The attack requires no special privilege — any account holding enough of the relevant TRC10 token or TRX can call `ExchangeTransactionContract` to move the price, and the exchange price impact from a single large trade is entirely deterministic from the Bancor formula. Exchanges with shallow liquidity (typical for most TRC10 pairs, since anyone can `ExchangeCreateContract` a new pool) make this trivially cheap; larger/deeper pools require more capital but remain exploitable given no cap on trade size relative to reserves exists in `doValidate()` beyond the `dynamicStore.getExchangeBalanceLimit()` ceiling on absolute balance, not price-impact percentage.

### Recommendation
Introduce manipulation resistance for the built-in `Exchange`: enforce a maximum per-transaction price-impact percentage (reject trades that move the ratio beyond a configurable bound), and/or compute a time-weighted price over a window of blocks for anything that treats the exchange ratio as a reference price, rather than relying on the single-block instantaneous reserve ratio computed in `ExchangeCapsule.transaction()`.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker (or colluding accounts) calls `ExchangeCreateContract`/observes an existing low-liquidity `Exchange` pool for TokenA/TRX.
2. Attacker submits `ExchangeTransactionContract` selling a large amount of TokenA into the pool (`ExchangeTransactionActuator.execute`, `chainbase/.../ExchangeCapsule.java:124-146`), sharply reducing `secondTokenBalance` (TRX) relative to `firstTokenBalance` and thus crashing the TRX-denominated price of TokenA in that pool.
3. A victim (or the attacker's own second account) executes a trade against the now-skewed pool, receiving far less TRX than fair value for their TokenA, or the attacker performs the reverse trade to redeem the artificially cheap TRX/TokenA, then withdraws the pool's TRX via `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` (creator-only) if attacker is also the pool creator, or simply keeps the arbitrage profit from the sandwiched counter-party trade.
4. No on-chain check in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` (`actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeTransactionActuator.java:149-224`) limits the price impact of a single trade beyond the trader's own self-supplied `tokenExpected` minimum, confirming the missing manipulation-resistance control.
