### Title
`ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` compute counter-token amounts from the live bancor-style pool ratio with no caller-supplied minimum/maximum bound, enabling sandwich extraction — (File: `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java`, `actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java`)

### Summary
`ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` let an exchange's creator add or remove liquidity from a TRX/TRC10 bancor-style pool (`ExchangeCapsule`). Both actuators derive the amount of the *other* token to move (`anotherTokenQuant`) purely from the pool's current on-chain balance ratio at execution time, and unlike `ExchangeTransactionActuator` (which requires a caller-supplied `expected` minimum), neither contract exposes any minimum/maximum bound for `anotherTokenQuant`. This is structurally the same missing-slippage-protection pattern as the reported `_harvest` bug: a value derived from a manipulable, order-dependent pool state is used to move funds with no caller-controlled tolerance check.

### Finding Description
In `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute` (and mirrored in `doValidate`), the counter-token amount is computed straight from the pool ratio present in `ExchangeCapsule` at the moment the transaction executes: [1](#0-0) 

The validate path recomputes the same unProtected ratio and only checks that the account has enough balance, not that the ratio is within any tolerance the creator specified: [2](#0-1) 

`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` follows the identical pattern — `anotherTokenQuant` (the amount of the paired token removed from the pool and paid out) is derived from the live ratio with no user-supplied bound, only an internal "precision" tolerance unrelated to price manipulation: [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) 

Contrast this with `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, which does implement exactly the mitigation recommended in the external report — a caller-supplied `expected` amount that the trade must meet or the transaction reverts: [5](#0-4) [6](#0-5) 

The pool's ratio (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`) is directly moved by any account submitting an `ExchangeTransactionContract` against the same exchange, since `ExchangeTransactionActuator.execute` mutates `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` via `exchangeCapsule.transaction(...)`: [7](#0-6) 

Because Inject/Withdraw transactions are broadcast to the mempool before inclusion, any unprivileged account can observe a pending Inject/Withdraw transaction, submit an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap immediately before it to skew `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` in their favor, let the victim's Inject/Withdraw execute against the skewed ratio (forcing the exchange creator to deposit more of the paired token than fair value, or withdraw less than fair value), and then submit a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap afterward to restore the ratio and capture the difference — a textbook sandwich attack, the same MEV pattern described in the external report.

### Impact Explanation
A successful sandwich forces the exchange creator's Inject to pay in more of the counter-token than the pre-manipulation fair ratio implied, or forces their Withdraw to receive less than fair value; the attacker extracts the difference as risk-free arbitrage profit. This is a concrete unauthorized-value-extraction/theft-of-funds scenario reachable purely by broadcasting ordinary signed `ExchangeTransactionContract` and `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` transactions — no privileged role, SR/witness collusion, or off-chain component is required.

### Likelihood Explanation
Exchange creators (asset issuers who set up bancor pools) routinely call Inject/Withdraw to manage liquidity, and any account can watch the mempool and submit `ExchangeTransactionContract` transactions to move the ratio before/after such calls in the same or adjacent blocks. The attack requires no special privileges, only transaction fees, and the on-chain economic incentive (arbitrage profit vs. the victim's loss) makes it directly exploitable whenever an Inject/Withdraw transaction with meaningful size is visible in the mempool.

### Recommendation
Add caller-supplied slippage-tolerance fields (e.g., `expected`/min-`anotherTokenQuant` and max-`anotherTokenQuant`) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and validate the computed `anotherTokenQuant` against that bound in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator`, mirroring the `expected` check already present in `ExchangeTransactionActuator`.

### Proof of Concept
1. Exchange creator broadcasts `ExchangeInjectContract` to inject `tokenQuant` of `firstTokenID` into pool P, expecting `anotherTokenQuant` of `secondTokenID` to be pulled from their account at the current ratio (`firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance`).
2. Attacker observes this pending transaction and broadcasts an `ExchangeTransactionContract` swap against pool P with a higher fee/priority, shifting `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` so that the ratio used by the creator's Inject becomes unfavorable to the creator.
3. The creator's `ExchangeInjectContract` executes against the skewed ratio via `ExchangeInjectActuator.execute` (lines 71-83), causing them to give up more `secondTokenID` than fair value, with no `expected`/minimum check to abort the transaction.
4. Attacker broadcasts a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` to restore the ratio and realize the price difference as profit, analogous to the sandwich technique cited in the original report.
