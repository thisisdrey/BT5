## Finding

The java-tron on-chain Bancor-style exchange (`ExchangeCreateContract`/`ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract`/`ExchangeTransactionContract`) is a directly reachable analog. Notably, `ExchangeTransactionContract` was hardened with an `expected` slippage-protection field [1](#0-0)  that is enforced in `ExchangeTransactionActuator.doValidate()` [2](#0-1) , but `ExchangeWithdrawContract` has no equivalent minimum-output field [3](#0-2) .

### Title
Missing slippage/minAmountOut protection in ExchangeWithdrawActuator allows front-running of liquidity withdrawals - (File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeWithdrawActuator.java)

### Summary
`ExchangeWithdrawContract` lets an exchange creator withdraw one side of a bancor-style pool and receive a proportional amount of the other token, computed from the pool's live reserves at execution time. Unlike `ExchangeTransactionContract`, which carries an `expected` field enforced during validation, the withdraw contract has no analogous minimum-received parameter, so the actuator accepts whatever `anotherTokenQuant` the manipulated reserves yield.

### Finding Description
In `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()`, the amount of the paired token returned to the withdrawer is computed purely from the exchange's current `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` ratio at execution time: [4](#0-3) 

`doValidate()` only checks that the computed `anotherTokenQuant` is internally consistent/precise and that balances are sufficient — it never compares it to a caller-supplied minimum acceptable amount [5](#0-4) . Because reserves are shared, mutable state that any account can move by submitting an `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade against the same `exchange_id`, an attacker can observe a pending `ExchangeWithdrawContract` in the mempool, submit a large trade to skew the `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` ratio immediately before it, let the victim's withdrawal execute against the skewed ratio, and then reverse the trade afterward — a classic sandwich attack. This is the exact bug class from the referenced report: a pool-ratio-dependent settlement with no `minAmountOut`/slippage bound, executed atomically with no protection against reserve manipulation between signing and execution.

By contrast, `ExchangeTransactionContract` (buy/sell) already has the `expected` field and enforces `anotherTokenQuant >= tokenExpected` [2](#0-1) , showing the protocol designers recognized the need for slippage protection on ratio-dependent settlement — but never extended it to `ExchangeWithdrawContract` (or `ExchangeInjectContract`).

### Impact Explanation
An exchange creator withdrawing liquidity can receive significantly less of the paired token than the pool ratio implied when they signed and broadcast the transaction, while the attacker profits by capturing the spread — a direct, unauthorized value transfer between users of the same on-chain contract via transaction ordering, without needing any special node/witness/peer privilege.

### Likelihood Explanation
The `ExchangeWithdrawContract` is broadcastable by any account holding the exchange (the "creator" address) and is settled deterministically against public, front-runnable reserve state at block-application time in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute()`. Any observer of the transaction pool (a normal, unprivileged broadcaster) can construct and submit a preceding `ExchangeTransactionContract` to shift the ratio, then a trailing one to restore it — requiring only ordinary signed transactions, no privileged role.

### Recommendation
Add a `min_another_token_quant` (or similarly named) field to `ExchangeWithdrawContract` (and consider the analogous protection for `ExchangeInjectContract`), and enforce it in `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.doValidate()`/`execute()` the same way `tokenExpected` is enforced in `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, rejecting the transaction if the computed `anotherTokenQuant` falls below the caller-supplied minimum.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes a pending `ExchangeWithdrawContract` from victim V withdrawing `tokenQuant` of `firstTokenID` from exchange `E`.
2. Attacker submits `ExchangeTransactionContract` trading a large amount of `secondTokenID` into `E`, sharply increasing `secondTokenBalance` relative to `firstTokenBalance` (or vice versa depending on direction needed to disadvantage V).
3. V's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` executes in the next block/position, computing `anotherTokenQuant = secondTokenBalance * tokenQuant / firstTokenBalance` [6](#0-5)  against the now-skewed reserves, yielding far less value than expected, with no `doValidate()` check able to reject it since there is no minimum-output parameter [7](#0-6) .
4. Attacker submits a reversing `ExchangeTransactionContract` immediately after, restoring the ratio and pocketing the difference extracted from V.
