### No vulnerability found for this question.

The behavior described is explicit, documented, intended design rather than an exploitable flaw. The fee-refund promise created in `inner_rlp_execute` at [1](#0-0)  is deliberately dispatched as an independent promise batch (via `env::promise_batch_create`/`env::promise_batch_action_transfer`), separate from the `.then()`-chained action promise that leads to `rlp_execute_callback`. The comment directly above this block states this is intentional: the fee compensates the relayer "for sending the transaction," and explicitly warns "Users should always verify the fee before signing a base token transfer. Relayers should also verify the fee before sending to make sure the user's signed transaction will refund enough to cover the relayer's gas costs" [2](#0-1) .

Key reasons this fails the validation bar:
- The fee amount and the fact that it is unconditional is chosen and cryptographically authorized by the wallet owner themselves when they sign the RLP transaction (`fee` field). A relayer cannot forge or increase this value; it can only collect what the signer already agreed to pay [3](#0-2) .
- `rlp_execute_callback`'s `CallerDeposit` refund path exists for a different purpose — refunding the `NearToken` deposit attached to the top-level call when the wrapped action fails — not for reversing the separately-dispatched relayer fee, which was never intended to be tied to action success [4](#0-3) .
- There is no victim distinct from the signer: loss is bounded strictly to the `fee` value the account owner explicitly signed, and the scenario requires the owner's own signature over a transaction they control. This is a self-inflicted or accepted business-logic risk (analogous to paying gas/relay fees regardless of downstream call outcome), not an authorization bypass, double-spend, inflation, or freezing of third-party funds.
- No consensus, state-root divergence, or shard-halting impact is present; this is a scoped, single-account economic design tradeoff that the contract authors explicitly documented and shifted responsibility for onto users/relayers to verify before acting.
