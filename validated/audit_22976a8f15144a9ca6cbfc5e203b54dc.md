### Title
AMM/Exchange spot-price used directly as trade rate with no slippage protection in `ExchangeInject`/`ExchangeWithdraw` enables sandwich/price-manipulation theft - ([File: actuator/src/main/java/org/tron/core/actuator/ExchangeInjectActuator.java])

### Summary
The TRC10 on-chain `Exchange` (a Bancor-relay style AMM) computes conversion ratios purely from the current mutable pool reserves (`firstTokenBalance` / `secondTokenBalance`) at execution time. `ExchangeInjectActuator` and `ExchangeWithdrawActuator` derive the counter-token amount (`anotherTokenQuant`) directly from this instantaneous ratio and, unlike `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, provide no caller-supplied minimum-output ("slippage") guard. This mirrors the EraLend bug class: a price derived from a manipulable on-chain reserve ratio is trusted instantaneously by a value-transferring operation, letting an attacker skew the ratio right before a victim's inject/withdraw executes and reverse it afterward, extracting the difference (a sandwich attack functionally equivalent to flash-loan oracle manipulation).

### Finding Description
`ExchangeCapsule.transaction()` and the actuators all read `firstTokenBalance`/`secondTokenBalance` straight from the live `ExchangeCapsule` state to compute exchange rates [1](#0-0) . These reserves are themselves fully attacker-controllable in the same block: any account can call `ExchangeTransactionContract` (via `ExchangeTransactionActuator`) with a large TRX/token amount to swing the pool ratio arbitrarily far, bounded only by `getExchangeBalanceLimit()` [2](#0-1) .

`ExchangeInjectActuator.execute` then computes the paired token amount required/returned using only that live ratio, with no bound on how much the ratio may have moved relative to the caller's intent: [3](#0-2) 

Likewise, `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` computes `anotherTokenQuant` (the amount of the *other* token the withdrawer receives) purely from the current ratio at execution time: [4](#0-3) 

Neither contract exposes an `expected`/minimum-output field to the caller, unlike `ExchangeTransactionActuator`, which explicitly validates `anotherTokenQuant >= tokenExpected` to protect the trader from price movement between submission and execution: [5](#0-4) 

Because `ExchangeInjectContract`/`ExchangeWithdrawContract` lack this protection, an attacker who can order transactions within a block (or simply race a victim's pending inject/withdraw) can:
1. Submit a large `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade to push the pool ratio far from its fair value.
2. Let the victim's `ExchangeInject`/`ExchangeWithdraw` execute against this skewed ratio, causing the victim to receive far less (or the attacker's own withdraw to receive far more) of the counter-token than the fair-value ratio would provide.
3. Submit a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` trade to restore the ratio, realizing the extracted value.

This is structurally the same bug class as EraLend: a price feed with no manipulation resistance is trusted instantaneously by a value-moving operation, letting a single actor (using only ordinary signed transactions available to any broadcaster) move the "price" and profit from a counterparty's now-unprotected trade.

### Impact Explanation
Successful exploitation directly transfers value out of the `Exchange` pool (and the counterparties injecting/withdrawing liquidity) to the attacker — an unauthorized transfer of TRX/TRC10 asset balances, i.e., concrete theft of funds from the affected `ExchangeInject`/`ExchangeWithdraw` participants and/or the pool reserves themselves. Since TRC10 tokens and TRX balances are directly moved via `AccountCapsule.setBalance`/`reduceAssetAmountV2`/`addAssetAmountV2` in these actuators, the loss is realized on-chain immediately and cannot be reversed once mined.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any unprivileged account can broadcast `ExchangeTransactionContract`, `ExchangeInjectContract`, and `ExchangeWithdrawContract` transactions — no special privileges are required. Reordering/sandwiching transactions within a block is a well-understood technique (front-running via fee/ordering), and TRON's exchange pools with limited liquidity make ratio manipulation cheap. The lack of a minimum-output parameter on `ExchangeInject`/`ExchangeWithdraw` (contrasted with the protection already present on `ExchangeTransactionActuator`) indicates this is a genuine gap rather than an intentional design choice.

### Recommendation
Add a caller-specified minimum/maximum acceptable counter-token amount (slippage bound) to `ExchangeInjectContract` and `ExchangeWithdrawContract`, and validate it in `ExchangeInjectActuator`/`ExchangeWithdrawActuator` the same way `tokenExpected` is validated in `ExchangeTransactionActuator`. Consider also bounding per-transaction ratio movement or requiring a time-weighted reference price for large single-block swings.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker observes a pending `ExchangeWithdrawContract` from victim V on exchange pool P (firstToken A / secondToken B).
2. Attacker submits `ExchangeTransactionContract` selling a large amount of A into P, moving the A:B ratio sharply in their favor (bounded only by `getExchangeBalanceLimit`).
3. V's `ExchangeWithdrawContract` executes against the skewed ratio via `ExchangeWithdrawActuator.execute` (lines 74-89), receiving far less B than fair value for the A withdrawn.
4. Attacker submits a reverse `ExchangeTransactionContract` selling B back into P, restoring the ratio and realizing the B difference extracted from V's withdrawal, net of two `ExchangeTransactionActuator` fees (`calcFee()` = configurable, often 0/small).
