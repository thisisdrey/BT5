### Title
Meta-transaction sender (delegate) can steal the relayer-funded deposit by causing the inner action to fail on the receiver shard - (File: `runtime/runtime/src/actions.rs`)

### Summary
NEAR's meta-transaction feature (NEP-366, `DelegateAction`/`DelegateV2`) is the direct analog of the report's "delegator" role: the `sender_id` of a `DelegateAction` freely chooses the inner actions/receiver and gets its intent executed, while a different, unprivileged-relative party (the relayer) pre-pays all gas and deposit costs. Just like a delegation caveat "enforcer" chosen by the delegator, the inner receipt built from the `DelegateAction` is deliberately structured so the `sender_id` (the delegator) — not the relayer who paid — is the one who benefits from failure-path refunds, matching the report's core theme of a lesser-privileged party abusing an asymmetric trust/fee relationship for personal gain.

### Finding Description
When a relayer submits a meta transaction, `apply_delegate_action` builds a new inner `ActionReceipt` whose `predecessor_id` is set to the `sender_id` from the `DelegateAction` (the delegate/sender, e.g. "Alice"), while all gas/deposit costs for the whole delegate action, including the inner actions' attached deposits, were charged to the relayer at transaction-conversion time on the relayer's shard: [1](#0-0) 

Deposit refunds are routed to `receipt.balance_refund_receiver()`, which defaults to the receipt's `predecessor_id` unless `refund_to` was explicitly set: [2](#0-1) 

If the inner action fails to execute on the receiver's shard (e.g. `Bob`), `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` refunds the full attached deposit to `receipt.balance_refund_receiver()`, i.e. to the `predecessor_id` of that inner receipt — which is `sender_id` (Alice), not the relayer who funded the deposit: [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) 

This exact asymmetry is explicitly documented in the codebase's own architecture notes, which describe it as a known, unresolved trust issue rather than something structurally prevented: [5](#0-4) 

The pattern mirrors the external report precisely: a lower-trust party (`sender_id`/delegate, analogous to the “delegator”) fully controls the parameters of an operation (choice of receiver, actions, and — crucially — whether/how it fails) whose costs are borne by a higher-trust party (the relayer, analogous to the party executing the delegation chain), and can deliberately manufacture a failure to redirect funds to themselves.

### Impact Explanation
An `sender_id` who signs a `DelegateAction` can attach a large deposit to an inner action targeting a receiver/method they know (or arrange) will fail — e.g., a nonexistent method, a contract that reverts on that specific call, or timing the call so a precondition on the receiver fails. Because the deposit refund goes to the `predecessor_id` (`sender_id`) of the newly created inner receipt rather than to the relayer who actually paid for it, the relayer's funds are transferred to the delegator despite the relayer never intending to give them anything beyond gas/service fees. This is a direct fund-theft vector against relayers, which is the class of loss (concrete theft of funds) explicitly in scope.

### Likelihood Explanation
This requires no special privilege: any account holder can act as a `DelegateAction` sender and construct actions/args designed to fail against a chosen receiver while carrying a large deposit. The only prerequisite is a relayer willing to cover deposit/gas for the delegate action — an inherent part of how NEP-366 relayers are supposed to operate. The scenario is explicitly called out in the project's own documentation as a "financial incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached but will fail on Bob's shard," indicating the developers are aware of the mechanism but have not implemented a protocol-level mitigation (e.g., routing such refunds to the relayer, or requiring `refund_to` to be relayer-controlled by default for delegate-spawned receipts).

### Recommendation
- For receipts spawned from a `DelegateAction`, consider making the deposit-refund receiver the original transaction signer (relayer) by default rather than `sender_id`, unless the relayer explicitly permits otherwise (analogous to `refund_to_account_id`/`promise_set_refund_to` semantics already present in the protocol).
- Alternatively, require/encourage relayers to use `refund_to` overrides when constructing/forwarding delegate-action receipts, and document this as a hard requirement (not just a warning) for safe relayer implementations.
- As a stop-gap, relayers should simulate/dry-run the inner actions before submitting to reduce (but not eliminate) exposure, since this is a documented but unmitigated protocol-level design gap.

### Proof of Concept
1. Alice has zero NEAR balance and a relayer willing to sponsor meta transactions for her.
2. Alice crafts a `DelegateAction` with `sender_id = Alice`, `receiver_id = Bob`, containing a `FunctionCall` action to a nonexistent method on `Bob` (or one guaranteed to fail, e.g. `assert!(false)` gated by a condition Alice can control) with a large `deposit`.
3. Alice signs it and forwards it to the relayer, who wraps it in a `SignedTransaction` with itself as signer/payer, per the standard meta-tx flow (see `integration-tests/src/tests/features/delegate_action.rs` and `test-loop-tests/src/tests/meta_tx.rs` for the exact transaction construction pattern used in this repo's own tests).
4. The relayer submits the transaction; the relayer's balance is decremented by gas + deposit costs as `apply_delegate_action` treats the relayer as the payer of `prepaid_send_fees`/deposit.
5. On Bob's shard, the inner `FunctionCall` fails (method not found or explicit revert). `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` is invoked with `deposit_refund = total_deposit` because `result.result.is_err()`, and the refund receipt's beneficiary is `receipt.balance_refund_receiver()`, which resolves to `predecessor_id = Alice` (the `sender_id` from the `DelegateAction`), not the relayer.
6. Alice's account balance increases by the deposit amount that was paid for by the relayer, net theft of relayer funds equal to the deposit amount minus fees.

Note: I was unable to execute this scenario live (no filesystem/terminal access in this mode); the flow above is derived directly from the cited source and the project's own architecture documentation, which independently confirms the refund-direction behavior and names it as a known relayer risk.

```rust
        self.refund_to().as_ref().unwrap_or_else(|| self.predecessor_id())
```

```markdown
## Balance refunds in meta transactions

Unlike gas refunds, the protocol sends balance refunds to the predecessor
(a.k.a. sender) of the receipt. This makes sense, as we deposit the attached
balance to the receiver, who has to explicitly reattach a new balance to new
receipts they might spawn.

In the world of meta transactions, this assumption is also challenged. If an
inner action requires an attached balance (for example a transfer action) then
this balance is taken from the relayer.

The relayer can see what the cost will be before submitting the meta transaction
and agrees to pay for it, so nothing wrong so far. But what if the transaction
fails execution on Bob's shard? At this point, the predecessor is `Alice` and
therefore she receives the token balance refunded, not the relayer. This is
something relayer implementations must be aware of since there is a financial
incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached
but will fail on Bob's shard.
```
